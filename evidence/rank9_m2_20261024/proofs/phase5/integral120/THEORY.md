# Dimension 120: forcing weak group-theoreticality and eliminating one descent

This file records new phase-five arguments for `R9_2_12` and `R9_2_959`.
They concern arbitrary complex fusion categories, not only unitary or braided
categories. No earlier phase or online repository is changed. The finite
hypotheses are checked by `verify_reductions.py` using the original input.

## 1. A fermion-parity criterion

**Proposition.** Let C be an integral fusion category with commutative
Grothendieck ring, trivial universal grading, and dimension N divisible by
four. Write the induction of the unit into Z(C) as a sum of distinct simple
objects. Suppose exactly one nonunit summand has odd dimension, and that its
dimension is a positive power of an odd prime. Then Z(C) contains a
nontrivial Tannakian subcategory.

**Proof.** Integrality gives a canonical positive spherical structure and
makes Z(C) an integral nondegenerate braided category. The odd-prime-power
summand of I(1) and ENO Corollary 7.2 produce a nontrivial symmetric fusion
subcategory. If that symmetric category has dimension larger than two,
Deligne's theorem gives a nontrivial Tannakian subcategory. A symmetric
category of dimension two is either Tannakian or sVec. It remains to handle
sVec, generated by a fermion chi in Z(C).

Here is a short proof of the required dimension count. By Ostrik
Proposition 2.8, for the unnormalized modular S-matrix,

    S [I(1)] = N [I(1)].

The fermion chi has twist -1, whereas every summand of A=I(1) has twist +1
(Ostrik Theorem 2.5); hence chi is absent from A. The chi entry of this
identity is zero. For each simple Z in A, monodromy with the order-two
invertible chi is a scalar epsilon_Z in {+1,-1}, and
`S_{chi,Z}=epsilon_Z FPdim(Z)`. Thus the total dimension of the
constituents of A centralizing chi equals that of the other constituents,
namely N/2. This uses no braiding on C.

For completeness the same dimension count has the following independent
relative-center derivation. Let F:Z(C)->C be the forgetful functor and
g=F(chi). Because the universal grading of C is trivial, the forgetful homomorphism on invertible
objects is injective: its kernel is the group of tensor automorphisms of
Id_C, namely the characters of the universal grading group. Thus g is a
nontrivial order-two invertible, and D=<g> has dimension two.

Use the relative-center construction from the proof of ENO Lemma 9.17.
The forgetful functor Z(C)->Z_D(C), which remembers the half-braiding only
against D, is dominant. Its right adjoint on the unit gives a connected
algebra B contained in A, of dimension N/FPdim(D)=N/2. This follows either
from that lemma's dimension calculation or from the dominant-functor
index formula and FPdim(Z_D(C))=FPdim(C) FPdim(D).

We claim that B is precisely the sum of those constituents of A that
centralize chi. Here is the explicit morphism check, so this identification
does not require a fixed-algebra theorem or a choice of associator.
For a central simple Z occurring in A, take a nonzero map f:F(Z)->1.
It is a morphism to the unit in the relative center precisely when

    (id_g tensor f) c_{Z,g} = f tensor id_g.

The naturality of chi's half-braiding gives

    (f tensor id_g) c_{g,Z} = id_g tensor f.

Consequently the relative-center condition is equivalent to

    (f tensor id_g) (c_{g,Z} c_{Z,g}) = f tensor id_g.

In Z(C), Z tensor chi is simple, so this monodromy is scalar. Since chi
has order two, its monodromy eigenvalue is +1 or -1. The displayed condition
therefore holds exactly when Z centralizes chi. This proves the claim,
including multiplicities (which are one because the based ring is
commutative).

The dimension of B is N/2, which is even. B contains the unit, and every
nonunit summand of A except the specified odd-prime-power object has even
dimension. Parity therefore forces that odd object to occur in B. It lies
in the centralizer E=<chi>' inside Z(C). By nondegeneracy of Z(C), the
Muger center of E is exactly <chi>=sVec, so E is slightly degenerate.
ENO Proposition 7.4 says that a slightly degenerate integral braided
category containing a simple object of odd-prime-power dimension has a
nontrivial Tannakian subcategory. This proves the proposition. QED.

The argument explicitly treats a possible central fermion. It does not
infer a Tannakian category merely from an unspecified symmetric category.

## 2. Application to both dimension-120 tensors

Both input tensors have dimensions `(1^4,2^2,6^3)`, are commutative, and
have trivial universal grading. Their formal codegrees, with multiplicity,
are

    120, 24, 20, 30, 30, 6, 6, 4, 4.

Ostrik's induction theorem (Theorem 2.13 of arXiv:1309.4822) gives a
multiplicity-free decomposition of A=I(1) with simple dimensions

    1, 5, 6, 4, 4, 20, 20, 30, 30.

There is exactly one odd nonunit summand, of dimension five. The proposition
therefore forces a nontrivial Tannakian subcategory of Z(C).

Li Dai's Lemma 3.1 then implies that C is weakly group-theoretical. Its
full hypothesis is exactly: an integral category of dimension 120 whose
center contains a nontrivial Tannakian subcategory. For clarity, its
reduction is as follows. If the Tannaka group's order is a power of two,
it contains a quotient C2, and the corresponding Rep(C2) in the center
gives an extension or equivariantization of a dimension-60 category.
All dimension-60 categories are weakly group-theoretical by ENO
Theorem 9.16. If the Tannaka group's order is divisible by three or five,
the neutral component of the de-equivariantized center has dimension
`(120/|G|)^2`, with at most two prime divisors, and is solvable by ENO
Theorem 1.6. The extension/equivariantization and center characterizations
of weak group-theoreticality finish the reduction.

**New conclusion:** every hypothetical categorification of either
`R9_2_12` or `R9_2_959` is weakly group-theoretical. This conclusion applies
to arbitrary complex fusion categories and is not a model construction.

## 3. An auxiliary dimension-30 obstruction

**Lemma.** An integral fusion category of dimension 30 has no simple
object of dimension three.

**Proof.** ENO Theorem 9.2 makes every integral fusion category of
square-free dimension 30 group-theoretical. Every group G of order 30
has a normal cyclic subgroup P of order 15. One elementary proof is:
if its Sylow-five subgroup were not normal, there would be 24 nonidentity
order-five elements. Counting forces the Sylow-three subgroup to be
normal. The inverse image of the normal Sylow-five subgroup in the
order-ten quotient is then a normal subgroup of order 15, necessarily
cyclic, making its Sylow-five subgroup normal after all. Thus Sylow five
is normal. In the order-six quotient, the Sylow-three subgroup is normal;
its inverse image is the desired cyclic subgroup of order 15.

In a group-theoretical presentation C(G,omega,H,psi), simples over HgH are
indexed by projective irreducible representations of K=H intersect gHg^{-1},
with dimension `[H:K] dim(pi)`. Every such K has square-free order, so
H^2(K,C*)=0: restriction to a Sylow subgroup is injective on the
corresponding primary part by restriction/corestriction, while every
Sylow subgroup is cyclic and has trivial complex Schur multiplier.
Thus all projective degrees coincide with ordinary character degrees.

Set Q=H intersect P. It is cyclic and, being a subgroup of the cyclic
normal subgroup P, is normal in G. We have [H:Q]<=2, and Q lies in every
intersection K. Hence [H:K]<=2. If [H:K]=2, K=Q is cyclic, so every
projective irreducible of K has dimension one. If [H:K]=1, K=H has a
cyclic normal subgroup of index at most two. Restriction of an ordinary
irreducible representation to that cyclic subgroup is a sum of characters.
Clifford theory gives irreducible dimensions at most two: an orbit of size
two induces a two-dimensional representation, while an invariant character
extends across the cyclic quotient and gives one-dimensional representations.
Thus all resulting simple dimensions are one or two.
In particular no dimension-three simple occurs. QED.

This argument allows arbitrary three-cocycles and subgroup cochains; an
untwisted character-table search alone would not suffice. The elementary
group-of-order-30 fact can equivalently be checked on the four groups of
order 30. It is also a consequence of the classification of groups of
square-free order at this particular order.

## 4. No solvable category has dimension type (1^6,3^6)

**Theorem.** A fusion category D with six invertibles and six simple
objects of dimension three is not solvable.

**Proof.** Its dimension is 60. For each dimension-three simple X,
G[X] has order dividing both |G(D)|=6 and FPdim(X)^2=9. In the decomposition
of X tensor X*, all noninvertible summands have dimensions divisible by
three, so |G[X]| is divisible by three. Hence |G[X]|=3. The group of
six invertibles has a unique subgroup H of order three, so this same H
fixes all six noninvertible objects. It lies in D_ad. The remainder of
X tensor X* has dimension six, so D_ad is nonpointed.

Consider a nontrivial faithful grading with group order u. Each component
has dimension 60/u. The neutral component contains H and at least one
three-dimensional object. A component with a invertibles and b
three-dimensional simples has dimension a+9b. There are six invertibles
and six three-dimensional simples in all. These constraints leave only
u=2, with both components of type `(1^3,3^3)`. This elementary enumeration
is replayed exhaustively by the verifier. The neutral category would
have dimension 30 and a three-dimensional simple, contrary to Section 3.
Thus U(D)=1.

If D were solvable, the final step of a cyclic-prime extension/
equivariantization construction could not be an extension. It must be
an equivariantization by C_p. Its central Rep(C_p) embeds into D and
supplies p invertibles, so p=2 or p=3.

For p=2, its nontrivial character acts freely on the six dimension-three
objects: their stabilizers under G(D) have order three. It also acts
freely on all six invertibles. De-equivariantization has type `(1^3,3^3)`
and dimension 30, again impossible.

For p=3, its character subgroup is H and fixes all six noninvertible
objects. The six invertibles form two free orbits. De-equivariantization
has dimension 20 and is pointed: each fixed three-dimensional simple
splits into three invertibles, and each free orbit of invertibles gives
one invertible. To justify the splitting, the endomorphism algebra of
the free module of a fixed simple is a twisted group algebra of C3;
H^2(C3,C*)=0, so it has three one-dimensional simple modules. Each has
underlying object X and normalized dimension 3/3=1.

Write this pointed category as Vec_K^omega, |K|=20. The equivariantizing
C3 acts on K by group automorphisms. Its invariant invertibles each
contribute exactly three invertibles in the equivariantization, again
because H^2(C3,C*)=0. Six invertibles of D would require exactly two
fixed elements of K. But K has a unique, hence characteristic, Sylow-five
subgroup, and the C3 action is trivial there because Aut(C5)=C4.
There are at least five fixed elements, a contradiction. QED.

This theorem does not exclude categories of that dimension type in
general: the group-theoretical category C(A5,1,C3,1) supplies that type.
The conclusion is nonsolvability, which is the needed restriction.

## 5. No solvable category has dimension type (1^2,2,3^2,6)

**Theorem.** A fusion category D of this type is not solvable.

**Proof.** A nontrivial faithful grading of a dimension-60 fusion category
has components of dimension at most 30. A simple object of dimension six
alone contributes 36 to its component, which is impossible. Hence U(D)=1.
If D were solvable, its last cyclic-prime step would be an
C2-equivariantization, since it has exactly two invertibles. Let g be the
nontrivial underlying character. It fixes the unique objects of dimensions
two and six. It exchanges the two dimension-three simples: the stabilizer
of a dimension-three simple has order dividing nine, so cannot contain an
involution. De-equivariantization therefore has three invertibles (one
from the pair of original invertibles and two from the fixed dimension-two
object) and three dimension-three simples (one from their pair, two from
the fixed dimension-six object). This is the forbidden dimension-30 type
`(1^3,3^3)` of Section 3. QED.

## 6. Central involution descents of the two source rings

For a hypothetical central Tannakian Rep(C2), the underlying involution
must be one of the following source labels (zero-based):

| Ring | Involution | Dimension type after de-equivariantization |
|---|---:|---|
| R9_2_12 | 1 or 2 | `(1^2,2,3^2,6)` |
| R9_2_12 | 7 | `(1^6,3^6)` |
| R9_2_959 | 8 | `(1^6,3^6)` |

The verifier checks every orbit of multiplication by each involution.
A free pair of d-dimensional objects gives one d-dimensional object
below; a fixed d-dimensional object splits into two objects of dimension
d/2. This follows from the algebra 1+g and H^2(C2,C*)=0, with all
categorical premises stated. Sections 4 and 5 rule out both dimension
types whenever the descending category is required to be solvable.

## 7. Nonsolvable weakly group-theoretical categories of dimension 120

**Lemma.** Every nonsolvable weakly group-theoretical fusion category of
dimension 120 is group-theoretical.

**Proof.** By definition it is Morita equivalent to a nilpotent category N,
built from Vec by successive faithful extensions by finite groups G_i.
The product of the orders |G_i| is 120. If every G_i is solvable, refine
each extension by a composition series into cyclic-prime extensions.
Then N, and hence the original category, is solvable, a contradiction.

Consequently one grading group is nonsolvable. A nonsolvable finite group
whose order divides 120 has a nonabelian composition factor A5: the only
possible proper nonsolvable order is 60, and its group is A5. If a grading
group has order 120, every other stage is trivial and N is pointed.
Otherwise an A5 extension uses a factor 60, leaving only a single factor
two. Up to deleting trivial stages, there are two possible orders.

First suppose Vec is extended by A5 and then by C2. The intermediate
category is P=Vec_{A5}^omega. Every invertible P-bimodule category, as a
left P-module category, has pointed dual. The classification of such
module categories (Naidu--Nikshych, Example 2.2) requires a normal abelian
subgroup H of A5. Its only possibility is H=1, so the left module is the
regular P-module, with 60 simples permuted transitively by A5. Each
component of any extension of P is such a bimodule, and has FP dimension
60. Its 60 equally dimensional simples therefore all have dimension one.
The extension N is pointed, for every omega and all coherent extension
data.

Second suppose Vec is extended by C2 and then by A5. The intermediate
category is P=Vec_{C2}^omega. Its center is pointed of rank four: its
simples are labeled by the two elements of C2 and projective irreducible
characters of C2, which are all one-dimensional since H^2(C2,C*)=0.
The group BrPic(P) is the group of braided tensor autoequivalences of
Z(P), and embeds into the permutations of its three nonunit objects.
For clarity, faithfulness follows from the classification of pointed
braided categories by metric groups: a braided tensor autoequivalence
fixing all simple objects is tensor isomorphic to the identity. Thus
BrPic(P) is a subgroup of S3 and is solvable. The homomorphism
`A5 -> BrPic(P)` of extension components is trivial, since A5 is simple
nonabelian. Every component is the regular P-bimodule and has two simple
objects. They have equal FP dimension and their squared dimensions sum
to FPdim(P)=2; both are invertible. Again N is pointed.

In all cases N is pointed, so the original category is group-theoretical.
QED. This is a dimension-120 statement with the nonsolvability hypothesis;
it does not assert that all dimension-120 categories are group-theoretical.

## 8. General exclusion of R9_2_12 and R9_2_959

**Theorem.** Neither ring admits a complex fusion categorification.

**Proof.** Suppose C categorifies either ring. Section 2 proves that C is
weakly group-theoretical. The independently audited phase-four theorem
`phase4/integral/GROUP_THEORETICAL_OBSTRUCTIONS.md` excludes every
group-theoretical categorification, including all associators and subgroup
cochains. Section 7 consequently forces C to be solvable.

Its universal grading is trivial by the exact input tensor. The final
step of a cyclic-prime construction of C must therefore be an
equivariantization. Since C has four invertibles, the prime is two, and
the canonical central Rep(C2) has one of the underlying involutions
listed in Section 6. Its de-equivariantization D is solvable, being the
preceding category in this construction. It has one of the two dimension
types excluded for solvable categories by Sections 4 and 5. This is a
contradiction. QED.

Every reduction concerns an arbitrary hypothetical categorification.
The proof does not assume a fiber functor, braiding, unitarity, or an
uncited classification of all dimension-120 categories. It uses the
phase-four all-cocycle GT theorem only within its established scope.
